# Mara Bell’s Journal

## What I Was Doing While Everyone Else Planned the Fortress

Today, the town received a defensive blueprint.

It had circles on it.

Not the sort of circles children draw around a house when they are pretending the house is a castle. These were measured circles. They had labels. They overlapped in the middle and appeared to believe this was a victory.

The Church stood at the center of the drawing. The Barracks stood two tiles to the east. The Watchtowers watched the edges. The houses were arranged nearby so that people could rest without losing half their strength walking home.

Tovin held the paper above his head and announced that we had achieved a Spiritual and Kinetic Defense Zone.

I was behind the Church, trying to catch a chicken.

The chicken had escaped from the yard during breakfast. It had crossed the road, entered the hospital garden, and hidden under a cart. Three people had tried to catch it. All three had failed. The doctor had eventually shut the garden gate and told us the bird could remain there until it developed an interest in medicine.

I had been sent to retrieve it because I was twenty, quick on my feet, and still foolish enough to volunteer before asking why.

The chicken was not impressed by my qualifications.

While I was crawling under the cart, I heard Tovin explaining priest survival probabilities to Hester. He spoke of radii, range, and a fifty percent savior roll. Hester asked whether the plan included enough food for the priests.

Tovin said the plan included an optimal placement strategy.

Hester asked again about food.

I caught the chicken by the left leg.

It objected with more force than its size suggested. I held on. The bird flapped into my face, scratched my cheek, and left a feather in my mouth. The entire hospital garden applauded when I brought it back.

That was when the blueprint meeting moved to the tavern.

I followed because Hester had promised soup.

The paper was spread across the largest table. Someone had put a cup on the corner to stop it curling. The cup was empty, which seemed to me the most practical contribution anyone had made.

The drawing showed the Church, Barracks, Watchtowers, residences, and what Tovin called “the origin.” I thought origin meant the place where the town began. Tovin said it meant the center from which all sensible distances should be measured.

“Who decides where the origin is?” I asked.

“The plan does,” he said.

This was not an answer, but everyone nodded as if it had been printed in a book.

The Church and Barracks were close enough for guards to protect priests during a failed rite. The homes were close enough for settlers to rest near their assigned residences in the evening. The towers marked the perimeter. The walls made a clean outer line.

It was a fine plan.

Then I noticed that the road to the tavern ran straight through the middle of the “safe zone” and ended beside the woodpile.

“What happens when the cart comes through?” I asked.

“Which cart?”

“The one carrying the wood.”

Tovin looked at the map.

“The cart will follow the access route.”

“There is no access route.”

“There is an access intention.”

The room became quiet.

Hester took the blueprint, folded it once, and put it beneath the short leg of the tavern table.

It worked better there.

Later, the priest came looking for it. He had heard that the plan placed him within range of the Barracks and wanted to know whether anyone had considered where he would stand during the actual exorcism.

Tovin said the circles had considered it.

The priest asked whether the circles could carry a lantern.

Nobody answered.

I spent the afternoon hauling wood, repairing the garden gate, and returning the chicken to a place where it could not escape without witnesses. I also checked the wall near the east side. The stones were sound. The gate opened. The Watchtower stood where it had been built, contributing its quiet strength and refusing every suggestion that it should begin speaking.

At dusk, I sat outside my house.

The Church bell rang. Someone called for a missing hammer. A child ran past with a loaf of bread. Hester shouted from the tavern that anyone who had taken the good chair was to return it immediately.

The town looked nothing like the blueprint.

The blueprint looked nothing like the town.

Both were useful.

The paper showed where danger might come from and where people could protect one another. The town showed what protection was for. It was for chickens in hospital gardens. It was for tired workers near their homes. It was for priests who needed lanterns, guards who needed food, and twenty-year-old settlers who needed soup after chasing poultry through the mud.

I think the circles are important.

I also think someone should draw a chicken on the map.

It is the only creature in town that has breached every defensive perimeter without once declaring war.
