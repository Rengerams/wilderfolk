First Year in Wilderfolk

The journal of Mara Bell, who arrived at twenty and learned that confidence is no substitute for a roof

I began this journal because I thought a record would make the first year seem orderly.

It did not.

The pages filled with mud, meal times, names, weather, arguments, repairs, and warnings I had written down only after ignoring them. Still, the record helped. When I turned twenty-one, I could look back and see the shape of the year. The valley had taught me its rules by letting me break the small ones first.

This is what happened.

Day One: The road ended

I arrived on a morning when the sky looked scrubbed with a dirty cloth. The road came down from the hills, crossed a stream, and stopped. No milestone marked the place. No official stood waiting. The road simply gave up.

I was twenty years old. I had a borrowed coat, two good boots, a small knife from my mother, and the firm belief that confidence counted as preparation.

That belief lasted until lunchtime.

Old Tovin said we had reached the valley. Tovin had been there three days longer than me, which made him an authority. He had named the stream, the hill, two crows, and the mud that had taken the cart’s wheel. He claimed the crows were cousins. The crows kept their counsel.

The settlement was a collection of tired people carrying more confidence than supplies. We had open ground, scattered trees, wildlife at the edges, and no agreement about what should be built first.

I learned the first rule before sunset. Nothing begins finished. A house is a promise with walls. A workplace is an empty answer waiting for a worker. A road is a suggestion made from mud.

We built a house that evening. I helped carry the roof beams. I dropped one.

“I did not,” I said.

“It landed on your foot,” Bram told me.

“That proves I was standing in the right place.”

By nightfall, the house had walls, a roof that wanted to be a roof, and no proper assignment for the people meant to sleep there. I thought this was a poor beginning.

The next morning, everyone called it progress.

Days Two to Seven: I learned where people went

On the second day, I learned that a building could be complete and still useless. The workplace needed a worker. The house needed a resident. The worker needed a route. The resident needed food.

The town ledger showed numbers. My eyes preferred people. I watched who walked a long way to work, who returned home, who waited beside a building, and who appeared to have become involved in a conversation with a tree.

There was no shame in changing an assignment. There was shame in allowing a bad assignment to become a local tradition. I learned this after spending half a day carrying timber to a place where timber was no longer needed.

On the third day, we argued over a second workplace and a storehouse. We voted by shouting. The storehouse won when a sack of grain fell over and struck the youngest voter.

Storage is dull until it is needed. Then it becomes the most interesting building in the valley.

On the fourth day, I learned the meal windows. We gathered to eat between 08:00 and 10:00, then again between 18:00 and 20:00. I missed the morning meal because I wanted to finish a task. By noon my thoughts had become slow and pointed. By evening I had apologized to a fence.

The fence accepted.

On the fifth day, I understood that people did not work every hour simply because the schedule allowed it. They talked. They formed friendships. They courted, married, mourned, visited, and chose the worst possible moment to begin a difficult conversation.

This was not wasted time. The settlement was made of people before it was made of buildings.

On the sixth day, I looked outward. Deer moved at the edge of the map. Rabbits vanished into brush. The stream carried the valley’s water past our unfinished walls. The landscape was not empty ground between buildings. It was where food grew, danger approached, and roads found their shape.

On the seventh day, I looked back. The real plan was not what we had intended. It was what had survived the week.

The hours belonged to everyone

Our ordinary workday ran from 07:00 to 18:00. The schedule could be adjusted within a six-hour minimum and twelve-hour maximum. This seemed generous when I first heard it.

Eight hours was the neutral target. Beyond that, work followed a settler home. Schedule fatigue carried into the next day. A tired worker became less reliable, and reliability could fall as low as 0.65 of normal workplace output.

Hester explained this while I was trying to work an extra shift at the sawmill.

“You are making half a plank,” she said.

“It is a difficult plank.”

“It was a tree this morning.”

I stopped.

A well reduced ordinary energy loss to 0.8 times the base rate. Hardy settlers endured the day more easily. Fierce settlers pushed through shifts with unusual grit. None of these advantages abolished exhaustion. Even the toughest person in Wilderfolk eventually needed a chair.

The tavern kept its own hours, most useful from 17:00 to 23:00. The hotel served visitors through its own hospitality window. School followed the weekday work rhythm. I had assumed every building simply opened when it was built. The valley corrected me by leaving several important places standing empty.

The lesson took me most of the year to trust: a full schedule is not a successful schedule if everybody reaches tomorrow already tired.

Food, fire, and the winter that had been waiting

Winter did not arrive like an enemy. It arrived like a condition that had been present all along.

When the cold season came and a settler stood in a workplace or home that could not be heated, energy loss rose to 1.5 times normal. Greenthumb settlers resisted part of the cold penalty. They still looked cold. They merely suffered with greater botanical dignity.

Our standard storehouse held 800 units of wood. By late autumn I understood why the Wood Storehouse mattered. Fuel that seemed abundant in summer became a private argument with the frost.

The first winter rule was simple: bank wood before anyone began explaining that there was plenty of wood.

The second rule concerned blueberries.

Blueberry trees were rare. I found one on the edge of the settlement and marked it in my mind. That was a mistake. Everyone else found it too.

A ripe pick gave 4 food and 45 energy. A tree held up to six portions. One portion regrew every four days outside winter. During winter, the tree gave nothing back.

Free-time settlers who were hungry could forage nearby. Foraging did not replace meals, work, school, festivals, or urgent famine behavior. It was a cushion. It was never the whole economy, no matter how lovingly Tovin spoke to the tree.

My first blueberry harvest caused an argument between a farmer and a physician. The farmer said the berries belonged to the village. The physician said the berries belonged to whoever was most tired. The tree resolved the matter by running out.

Deer gave 52 food when hunted. Rabbits gave 22. The first deer made us feel rich. The second deer made us careless. The valley’s ecology noticed before our ledger did.

By late autumn, I checked food, wood, heating, and completed buildings before I checked anything that looked impressive. This was the beginning of wisdom. It was also the beginning of several boring evenings.

Houses and the hospital

A house became real when someone was assigned to it. I learned this after walking home to a building that had walls, a roof, and no one officially living there.

A completed assigned residence also gave a settler a better evening. When I rested near my own home, within roughly 14 world units, energy loss could fall to half the ordinary rate. The first time I noticed this, I stood outside my door and felt my body stop arguing with the day.

Distance cost more than I expected. A long walk spent time and energy. Homes placed near the places people used most often made the whole settlement feel less tired.

The hospital taught me a different lesson. It was not a roof with medical decorations. It needed a doctor. Pregnant settlers qualified for care. Settlers below 42% energy could need treatment. Grieving settlers might seek care below 70%.

A doctor could heal nearby patients, sometimes using one food as medicine. Every three days, the hospital performed ward rounds and treated a bounded number of people based on the hospital and its occupants.

When my neighbor lost someone, I asked when she would return to work.

Hester told me not to ask that again.

I wrote the sentence down and kept it.

The valley remembered its dead. That memory made the living harder to manage and more worth protecting.

The children and the school

A child called Pella asked whether school existed to prevent children from doing useful work.

The teacher said, “It exists to make useful work possible later.”

Pella considered this and then asked whether later could begin after lunch.

A staffed school could teach up to ten children. School time was counted during weekday work hours. Education accumulated through school days. Maturation and later graduation benefits came through established progression points rather than one grand lesson.

I liked this. It felt honest. Nobody became wise because a bell rang once.

The children also needed room to be children. A settlement could not treat them as small adults merely because food was short. Housing, care, school, and family ties shaped the people who would later work in the town.

Settlers arrived with different traits. Some were Hardy. Some were Fierce. Some were nurturing. Some belonged near plants, people, tools, or trouble. A trait was useful information, not a complete person.

I once assigned a settler to a job because the ledger made the choice look perfect. The settler lasted half a day, went home furious, and married the person I had assigned to the opposite end of town.

I never learned whether this was romance or revenge.

The moon’s schedule

The moon kept better time than we did.

Every fourteen days, it returned full. On a full-moon night, the dangerous hours began at 20:00 and ended at 06:00.

A cursed adult settler could transform into a Moon Howler. Children could not carry the curse. Visitors and rivals could not carry it either. This was a small mercy.

When the transformation began, the Howler was forced into the open. Job, residence, and certain building connections were detached for the hunt and restored afterward when capacity allowed.

The first time it happened, I thought the Church would solve everything because the Church was there.

The Church did not solve anything. It was unstaffed.

A staffed Church could attempt an exorcism when a priest stood within 200 world units of the Howler. A rite could be attempted every two hours during the cure window. One priest had a 35% cure chance. Each additional priest added 12 percentage points. With four priests, the working maximum for the Church was 71%. The code retains a 78% cap for higher priest counts, but that is not the normal four-priest Church case.

A failed rite could kill the priest or make the priest flee. A guard from a Barracks within 220 world units could make a separate 50% save roll against a fatal result.

This was not a guarantee. It was a second chance with a body count attached.

A Church without a priest on duty was a building. A Barracks without a guard was also a building. Buildings did not perform responsibilities. People did.

At dawn, a living Howler returned to human form. If the curse remained, the next full moon could bring the hunt again. A new Howler was not guaranteed every moon. Replacement was rare. The valley had many ways to frighten us and did not need to use the same one on schedule.

Bram announced three times that he was brave enough to face the Howler alone.

On the third announcement, he was the person being rescued.

Walls and the watchtower

A wall was a sentence written around the settlement: this is where we stop being easy to reach.

We placed the first gate badly. Everyone used it anyway. Later we placed a second gate. Everyone continued using the first one.

The Watchtower was a passive defensive structure. It added 15 barricade strength and could follow its existing forged-ballista upgrade path. It did not fire on anything merely because Tovin had drawn arrows on its plan.

“Perhaps it needs encouragement,” he said.

The tower remained quiet.

Defenses had to support the town rather than strangle it. A wall could keep danger out while making food, work, school, and emergency movement harder. The useful question was not how many walls we had. It was what happened when something reached them.

Who was nearby? Who was on duty? Where could a priest move? Where could a wounded person recover? Which gate would everyone use despite the other gate?

The answer remained the gate nearest the tavern.

Nobody knew why.

Neighbors, visitors, and rivals

By summer, the town had stopped feeling alone.

Visitors arrived with luggage, needs, and stories. The hotel could house them when properly staffed. The tavern gave everyone a place to gather after work and describe the day with fewer facts than it had begun with.

Rivals watched from beyond our borders. They had their own food, wood, gold, morale, recovery, and priorities. Each day they might gather, trade, fortify, scout, recover, or cool down. The Frontier panel showed their actions. It did not make their motives simple.

A rival that scouted was not necessarily declaring war. A rival that fortified was not necessarily afraid. A rival that cooled down might have chosen not to make the day worse.

Diplomacy often turned on meaning. A practical gift could look generous to one settlement and insulting to another. A delayed reply could mean caution or contempt. A wedding invitation could carry more danger than a raid because people brought opinions to weddings.

I learned to watch patterns instead of single acts.

The valley’s stories grew from these patterns. The guided campaign recorded five ordered chapters: The Deer Parliament, The Traveling Theatre Company, The Wedding That Nearly Started a War, The Apprentice’s Terrible Invention Fair, and The Rumour Ledger.

The names sounded theatrical. The consequences belonged to the settlement. Stewardship, public memory, diplomacy, invention, and truth all arrived wearing ordinary clothes.

The campaign did not replace the existing simulation. It watched for stories that had already become real and gave them a place in the town’s memory. The valley could interpret its history. It could not change the authoritative record because the dramatic version had better lighting.

Major stories arrived with space between them. Each was a one-time experience per save, selected through varied seeded opportunities and shared cooldowns. If three disasters, two weddings, and a theatrical deer appeared in one afternoon, someone had made a mistake with the calendar.

It was usually Tovin.

The end of my first year

My first year ended without a trumpet.

Someone repaired a roof. Someone checked the wood. Someone waited for the school bell. Someone carried food past the place where the first cart lost its wheel. Someone told a visitor that the settlement had always been this organized.

That person was me.

I had turned twenty-one. I still felt twenty whenever someone handed me a responsibility and looked away.

We had eleven settlers, one school, two unfinished houses, a hospital that had saved more people than anyone admitted, and a watchtower Tovin believed could speak. Pella brought him a ladder. He climbed it, looked over the valley, and said, “There. Now it speaks.”

The tower remained silent.

The settlement did not.

That was the part I had missed when I arrived. Surviving Wilderfolk was not defeating it. The valley answered the shape of our decisions. Food ran short because we had planned for today. Workers failed because we had mistaken endurance for strength. Children grew because people had made room for them. A hospital mattered because someone had suffered. A wall mattered because someone had watched the road.

I arrived at twenty with a borrowed coat and no useful experience.

I left my first year with a house that had a proper roof, a list of people I trusted, and a second coat that was actually mine.

This was enough to begin again.

Final entry

Keep food before winter. Keep wood before cold. Keep homes near people. Keep hospitals staffed. Keep schools from becoming storage sheds. Keep priests and guards near the danger they are meant to answer.

Keep one eye on the event log and the other on the person who says, “I have a plan.”

When the plan fails, make tea for the person beside you.

Then write down what happened.

Someone will need the guide next year.
