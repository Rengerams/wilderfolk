# Second Year in Wilderfolk

## The journal of Mara Bell, who learned that belonging is mostly knowing which roof leaks

I began my second year at twenty-one, which was old enough for people to ask my opinion and young enough for them to ignore it when I gave one.

The settlement had changed while I was busy surviving it. The first year had been a collection of emergencies tied together with string. The second year acquired habits. People checked the wood before the frost. They looked at the sky before promising a harvest. They counted beds before inviting visitors. They asked whether a building had a worker before praising its roof.

This was progress.

It was less exciting than the first year and much more useful.

I had a proper coat now. The left sleeve had been repaired twice. I considered this a sign of prosperity because the sleeve was still attached.

---

## The first morning of the second year

On the first morning, Hester asked me to inspect the houses near the eastern field.

“I am not a builder,” I said.

“You have eyes.”

“I have two.”

“Then use both.”

The houses stood where we had put them the previous autumn. One was sound. One leaned slightly toward the road. The third had a roof that looked confident from a distance and uncertain up close.

We had built it during a week when everyone wanted to finish before winter. That was how a building acquired a roofline like a tired eyebrow.

The residents had placed a bucket beneath the worst drip. The bucket was full. A child had floated a wooden button in it and named the vessel Captain.

I asked whether the leak had been reported.

The mother pointed at the bucket. “Captain has been promoted three times.”

We repaired the roof before lunch.

That was my first lesson of the year. A settlement remembers its unfinished work. It does not always complain loudly. Sometimes it places a bucket beneath the evidence and waits to see who notices.

---

## Work became a thing people discussed

In my first year, work had been a matter of finding an empty place and putting a person in it. By my second year, everyone had an opinion about the arrangement.

The ordinary workday still ran from **07:00 to 18:00**, and the schedule could be shortened or lengthened within its proper limits. Eight hours remained the neutral measure. We had learned that the extra hours did not vanish. They followed people home and sat down at the table.

A tired settler could still work. A tired settler could also carry one sack of grain to the wrong building, forget why they had entered it, and blame the building.

The sawmill received most of the blame.

Hester said a long shift had its place during a crisis. Bram said every day was a crisis if one looked at it correctly. Bram had never managed a workplace. He had managed to be near several workplaces while holding opinions.

We adjusted the schedule before the late spring work began. The farmers wanted longer days. The hospital wanted fewer exhausted patients. The school wanted children to arrive awake. The tavern wanted everyone to believe it had nothing to do with any of this.

For a while, the village argued over hours as if time were a sack of flour and someone had stolen a handful.

Then the first long day arrived.

By evening, two workers had gone home without eating. One had fallen asleep beside a fence. Another had stood in the middle of the road and announced that the road was moving.

The road was not moving.

We shortened the schedule the following morning. Production dipped. Tempers improved. The hospital had fewer people waiting for care. The schoolteacher stopped rubbing her forehead with both hands.

The ledger called this a reduction in output.

I called it an improvement in the number of people who knew where they were.

## We built roads and discovered that straight lines have opinions

The first proper road in the settlement began as a brown strip through the grass. It connected the storehouse to the houses and ended beside a tree that everyone insisted had been there first.

The road made travel easier. It also revealed that none of us knew how to draw a convincing bend.

The builder placed one straight section, then another. The two did not meet cleanly. A gap of grass remained between them, narrow as a lie.

“Recht one and Recht two are different,” Bram said, examining the pieces.

“They are both straight,” I said.

“That is why it is complicated.”

We tried a corner. It faced the wrong way. We rotated it. The corner faced a different wrong way with confidence.

Someone brought out a hand-painted set of path pieces. They showed straight lengths, bends, T-junctions, and a crossing. The pieces were warm ochre, edged with irregular grass, and a little too large in some places. At full view they looked lovely. At close range one could see every small disagreement between the grass and the dirt.

This did not stop us using them.

A path in Wilderfolk does not need to survive an inspection by a magnifying glass. It needs to lead a person from a house to a workplace without looking as if the ground has been cut with a pastry wheel.

We kept the two straight pieces as a pair. The bends could turn with the road. The T-junctions served where three paths met. The crossing became the proud centre of a road network that had four roads and one goat standing in the middle of it.

The goat refused to move until the road had been named.

It was named the North Road, although it went east.

The roads changed travel. They also changed the way the settlement looked at itself. Paths made intentions visible. A road from the houses to the farm said that food mattered. A road to the gate said that visitors and danger were both expected. A road that ended at the tavern said something about us that I chose not to write down until after supper.

The decorative path itself did not harvest, build, fight, or think. It was ground made legible. That was enough.

## The church had one roof and many opinions

The Church remained unique. This had become a matter of law, arithmetic, and repeated arguments from people who wanted to build another one because the first was too far away.

“One Church,” Hester said.

“What if we build two small ones?” Bram asked.

“That is two Churches.”

“What if one is only a chapel?”

“That is a Church wearing a smaller hat.”

The limit mattered most during the full moons. Four priests could bring the cure chance to its intended maximum of **71%**. More priests did not create a second miracle. They created a queue, an argument over stools, and at least one person asking whether the fifth priest could stand outside and contribute spiritually.

The answer was no.

A Moon Howler came in the second month. The cursed settler had been quiet for several days. People noticed the silence only after it had become difficult to ignore.

At 20:00, the hunt began.

The Church was staffed. The priests moved toward the Howler. The Barracks sent soldiers. The soldiers were no longer called guards in the ledger. This distinction pleased them more than I expected.

“A guard watches,” Bram said. “A soldier defends.”

“You are doing both,” I said.

“Yes, but the ledger should respect the difference.”

The Prison had its own guards now. Prison Guards stayed with the Prison. Soldiers stayed with the Barracks. This ended the old habit of assigning one person to a building and calling the arrangement good because both buildings had a person somewhere near them.

The rite failed on its first attempt.

A priest fell. A soldier reached him. The rescue was not certain. Nothing in the valley was certain merely because people had prepared for it.

The second attempt held.

The Howler returned to human form at dawn. The priest lived. The soldier who had carried him back complained that the priest was heavier than he looked.

The priest replied that wisdom had weight.

The soldier said wisdom should learn to walk.

The event stayed in the Chronicle. The rumor grew larger. By evening, three people claimed that the soldier had fought the moon itself. By the next day, one child said the moon had apologized.

The Chronicle kept the names and the facts. The settlers kept the better version.

Both were useful, provided nobody used the rumor to plan the next defense.

---

## Prisoners, soldiers, and the trouble with one word

The role split became most obvious in the Prison.

Before the change, a person listed as Guard could be assigned to a Barracks or a Prison. The ledger understood the word. People did not. A Prison Guard might be counted as part of the settlement’s defense. A soldier might be sent to watch a prisoner because someone had mistaken a shared label for shared duty.

The new names cured much of this.

A **Soldier** belonged to the Barracks. A **Prison Guard** belonged to the Prison. The Prison held prisoners, not workers with inconvenient sleeping arrangements. The Barracks held soldiers, not men who had been placed there because nobody knew where else to put them.

We still made mistakes.

On one wet afternoon, a newly assigned Prison Guard reported for duty at the Barracks. He stood under the gate and waited.

“What are you doing?” I asked.

“Guarding.”

“Where is the Prison?”

He looked over his shoulder. “I assumed it was behind me.”

It was. He had walked past it because the road bent.

The ledger had been correct. The road had been less helpful.

Once the assignments were corrected, the difference became plain. Barracks defense used soldiers. Prison staffing used Prison Guards. The Moon Howler response looked at the soldiers who could actually protect the rite. The Prison looked at the guards who could actually remain on site.

A name is a small wall. It keeps duties from wandering into the wrong building.

---

## The children grew while we were busy

Pella was taller by midsummer. She objected to this as if the school had done it without permission.

“I was comfortable before,” she said.

“You were also shorter than the desk,” the teacher told her.

“That was the desk’s fault.”

The school remained staffed and carried on with the patient business of making children more capable than they had been the year before. A school could teach up to **ten children**. It could not teach ten children to sit quietly when a deer appeared outside the window.

The deer had chosen the schoolyard because the grass was good there. The children chose to interpret this as a lesson.

The teacher chose to interpret it as a disruption.

Nobody won.

I began helping with the younger children twice a week. This was not because I had become wise. It was because Pella said the teacher needed someone who could reach the high shelf.

The children knew the settlement differently from the adults. They understood which paths became muddy, which houses smelled of bread, which settlers would share berries, and which adults could be distracted by a question about wolves.

They also knew that a new road could be used for races before it had been approved for anything else.

The village had a rule against running near the storehouse.

The children had a rule against obeying rules that had not yet been explained dramatically.

One afternoon, two of them raced from the school to the crossing. A third served as a judge. The judge disqualified both racers because one had “looked too confident.”

This was the first election held on the new road.

It was also the only one that ended before supper.

## The valley became crowded enough for manners

Visitors came more often in the second year. They arrived with goods, questions, tired animals, and opinions about our walls.

The hotel could receive them when staffed. The tavern could feed their stories when the evening service window opened. The market could turn reputation into prices, which meant that a good name was worth something until somebody said the wrong thing near a merchant.

One visitor asked whether our settlement had a leader.

We pointed toward the Town Hall.

“An elected leader?” she asked.

We pointed more carefully.

The election was still two years away. This did not prevent three people from preparing speeches.

Tovin promised to improve the western road, even though he had spent most of the year arguing that roads made people lazy. Bram promised to increase defense, though nobody could determine whether he meant more soldiers or a taller fence. Hester promised to repair whatever the others broke.

Her speech received the longest applause.

A traveling merchant arrived during a rainstorm and found the road to the market half finished. One wheel sank. The merchant blamed the road. The road received the accusation without expression.

The modder adviser arrived and spent twenty minutes explaining that the road had not failed. The ground had accepted the wheel too enthusiastically.

The merchant bought two sacks of grain and left with a written map of the driest route. The map showed a large warning mark beside the place where the wheel had sunk.

Someone later added a second mark because the first had not been sufficiently dramatic.

Visitors taught us that a settlement could be judged by people who had slept in it for one night. Rivals taught us that a settlement could be judged by people who had never entered it at all.

The Frontier reports became more detailed. Rival camps gathered, traded, fortified, scouted, recovered, and cooled down according to their own ledgers and priorities. A scout on the horizon was not automatically a declaration of war. A fortified camp was not automatically a threat. A quiet camp was not automatically friendly.

We learned to wait for patterns.

This was difficult. People prefer a single dramatic explanation. It fits better in conversation.

“The rival is preparing an attack,” Bram said after seeing a scout.

“The rival is looking at us,” I said.

“Why would anyone look at us unless they intended trouble?”

“Have you met us?”

He considered the question and decided it proved his point.

## The five stories found their places

The guided campaign continued in the background of the settlement, like a second journal kept by someone with better filing habits.

The Deer Parliament made people look at the animals around us with more care. The settlers called it a parliament because the deer stood together and stared at the fields as if waiting for a clerk. The ecology rules remained less poetic. Hunting pressure, food, wildlife, and grazing still answered to their own facts.

The Rumour Ledger made everyone nervous. A real event could become a story before the ink had dried. A family quarrel grew a second ending. A successful harvest acquired a heroic witness who had not been present. The event log kept the plain account. The tavern kept the version with weather, betrayal, and a goose.

The Traveling Theatre Company arrived late in summer. Their costumes were excellent. Their understanding of our history was not.

They performed a heroic account of the first winter in which I became, without consent, a woman who had carried a roof beam alone through a blizzard.

I had carried one end of a roof beam for twelve steps. The other end had been carried by Hester. The weather had been cold, but no snow had fallen.

When I corrected the troupe, the lead performer asked whether I preferred the accurate version.

I said yes.

She said, “Then you must understand why the audience prefers ours.”

I did understand. That was the irritating part.

The Chronicle remained unchanged. The play became a memory of how the settlement wanted to remember itself. We were not heroes. We had been wet, hungry, late, and determined not to admit how close the roof had come to falling on Bram.

The Apprentice’s Terrible Invention Fair produced a counting device that lost count whenever someone touched it. The apprentice called this a security feature. The grain keeper called it an insult.

The Wedding That Nearly Started a War never reached the catastrophe promised by its title. The invitation still caused three days of argument, one expensive gift, and a dispute over whether a delegation needed matching coats.

The Rumour Ledger recorded the dispute. By winter, people claimed the rival settlement had nearly attacked because our coats did not match.

The actual relationship remained intact.

This was the difference between a story and a state change. The story could grow horns. The state had to remain what it was.

## Éloi at the edge of the road

Near the end of summer, a strange workshop appeared at the edge of the settlement. Nobody admitted to building it. Éloi said the walls had assembled themselves around a sufficiently determined idea. Hester said he had paid a carpenter.

It sat beyond the last ordinary shed, where the road thinned and the grass began to reclaim the wheel marks. The building looked half like a barn and half like an argument with the future. Dark wood formed the walls. Metal showed around the windows. A narrow pipe rose through the roof and released a thread of blue-grey smoke whenever the wind allowed it.

A sign read:

**ATELIER RENAULT**  
**CRÉATIONS D’AUTOMOBILE**

Éloi Renault stood outside beside a low wooden vehicle with thick wheels. It was neither a cart nor a coach. It looked as if a wagon had grown tired of waiting for a horse and decided to take matters personally.

He watched me approach.

“You are looking at the wheels,” he said.

“I am trying to determine whether they are attached.”

“They are attached in principle.”

“That is not a reassuring category.”

Éloi had oil on his hands and a face made narrow by concentration. He spoke of vehicles that moved without horses. He spoke of roads that did not yet exist. He spoke of metal as though it were a stubborn animal that could be trained with enough patience.

The bridge warden disliked the weight of his machine. The mud adviser disliked the width of it. The schoolchildren liked the smoke. Éloi seemed pleased that all three had noticed.

A woman from the eastern houses stopped beside the yard. Éloi’s eyes followed her for half a second before he remembered that the vehicle was supposed to be the interesting thing.

“Do not stand in front of it,” he told her. “That is where the future has the least patience.”

She moved beside me. “Does the future have patience anywhere?”

“Not yet,” Éloi said.

The first trial took place at dusk. The machine shuddered. A gear clicked. One wheel turned. The vehicle rolled forward, stopped, rolled backward, and settled against a plank.

Nobody spoke.

Éloi lifted one finger.

“It moved in both directions,” he said.

The bridge warden closed his eyes. The woman beside me tried not to laugh. I failed first.

The vehicle was not part of the settlement’s transport system. It did not change the roads, the map, or the way people traveled. It was a local rumor with wheels and a workshop attached. The Chronicle had no reason to record it. The tavern recorded it anyway.

By morning, somebody had painted a small arrow on the road pointing toward the atelier.

The arrow pointed the wrong way.

Éloi said this was an early demonstration of public navigation. The mud adviser said it was an early demonstration of why roads required supervision.

## The winter did not care about our improvements

Winter came with the same old demands. Wood, food, shelter, heat, patience.

The settlement had more buildings now. This made the winter easier in some ways and more expensive in others. More people meant more work. More work meant more meals. More meals meant more food. More food meant everyone suddenly understood the importance of the harvest after it had already happened.

We had stored wood early. The Wood Storehouse was full enough to make people boast.

I stopped the boasting on the second cold morning by asking how many days the supply would last.

Nobody answered.

We checked the ledger.

The storehouse was still full. The confidence was not.

The hospital stayed staffed. The school remained open when the weather allowed it. The tavern grew louder as the nights grew longer. People came in from the cold and told stories about how well they had prepared.

One man claimed he had planned the winter before spring.

Hester asked why he had spent autumn looking for a missing boot.

He said the boot had been part of the plan.

A Moon Howler did not come that winter. We watched the full-moon nights anyway. Preparation has its own rhythm. It does not disappear because danger stays home.

The Church kept its four priests ready. The Barracks kept its soldiers separate from the Prison Guards. The Prison held its prisoners under watch. The walls stood where we had put them. The gate nearest the tavern remained the gate everyone used.

Some mysteries survive every improvement.

On the longest night, I walked the road from the houses to the Town Hall. Snow lay in the grass. The path showed dark beneath it. A light burned in one window. Someone inside was arguing about grain.

It was comforting.

A silent town would have frightened me more.

---

## Turning twenty-two

My birthday arrived during a thaw.

Hester gave me a pair of gloves with one thumb slightly higher than the other. She said this was because my hands were not symmetrical.

I wore them anyway.

Pella gave me a drawing of the settlement. The houses were square. The road was blue. The watchtower had a face. The deer had speech bubbles.

“What is the watchtower saying?” I asked.

“It is saying, ‘I told you so.’”

“About what?”

“Everything.”

The drawing showed Éloi’s workshop at the far edge of the map. His automobile had six wheels and a small flag. I asked why.

“It needs help turning,” Pella said.

The settlers gathered in the tavern that evening. Someone brought berries preserved from the previous year. Someone else brought a cake that had the shape of a wall corner. Nobody admitted who had made it.

Bram raised a cup.

“To Mara,” he said. “Who arrived with no experience and now has opinions about roofs, roads, hospitals, schools, prisons, soldiers, priests, rival camps, and the correct width of a path.”

“The path is too narrow,” I said.

“To Mara,” he repeated.

I had expected twenty-two to feel different. It did not. The number sat beside me like a quiet guest. It did not offer advice.

I looked around the tavern. The people were older by one year. The children were taller. The buildings carried more repairs. The village had more roads, more stories, more arguments, and more names for the same kind of mud.

It also had people who would come when a roof leaked.

That was new.

---

## The second year ends with a road that holds

At the end of the year, we repaired the eastern road.

The first version had failed at the bend. The grass kept appearing between the pieces. The path looked interrupted, as if it had remembered an appointment elsewhere.

We lifted the corner. We adjusted the straight sections. We used the pair that belonged together. We turned the bend until its edges met the road and its grass met the field.

The crossing remained slightly uneven.

Nobody cared.

A road does not become real because every edge is perfect. It becomes real when people use it without thinking. The farmer takes it toward the fields. The children race along it. A visitor follows it to the market. A soldier crosses it toward the gate. A Prison Guard walks past it to the Prison and does not accidentally end up at the Barracks.

The road holds because the settlement keeps choosing it.

I thought about that while walking home. The valley had not become kind. It still took food, heat, sleep, and attention. It still sent rain at the wrong time. It still allowed deer to stand in schoolyards and allowed machines to roll backward in front of witnesses.

But the valley had become familiar.

Familiarity is a dangerous comfort. It can make a person careless. It can also let a person notice the small things that matter. A bucket beneath a roof. A tired worker by a fence. A child waiting for the school bell. A priest standing too far from danger. A soldier who knows where he belongs. A path that reaches the house without breaking apart.

At twenty, I thought survival meant keeping disaster away.

At twenty-two, I understood that survival was also the work of making tomorrow easier to recognize.

I went home along the repaired road. The house still leaned slightly toward the east. Captain the bucket had been retired. The roof held.

For once, nothing needed fixing before morning.

I did not trust it.

I slept well.

## Final entry

Keep the wood ahead of the cold. Keep the food ahead of the argument. Keep workers near the work they can actually reach. Keep the Church unique. Keep Soldiers in the Barracks and Prison Guards in the Prison. Keep children away from the grain sacks unless you want a new kind of accounting.

Repair the road before people learn to walk around it.

Let the children draw the watchtower with a face.

Let Éloi promise that the machine will move forward next time.

Then check the bucket under the roof.

A year is not measured by the disasters it avoided. It is measured by the repairs that became ordinary, the names that became familiar, and the people who came running when something began to leak.

I arrived at twenty with a borrowed coat.

I turned twenty-two with gloves that fit badly, a repaired road outside, and a house that held through the night.

Tomorrow, someone will find a new leak.

I know where the bucket is.
